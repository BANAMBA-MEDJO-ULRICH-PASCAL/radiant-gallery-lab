## The problem

In Cameroon, internship offers circulate in WhatsApp groups. There is no filter,
no archive and no way to tell a real listing from a dead one — if you aren't in
the right group at the right hour, you simply never see it. On the other side,
small companies report that **most of the applications they receive are off
profile**: a tech firm advertising for a developer gets CVs from accountants.

LinkedIn does not solve this locally. It is built for people who already have a
career to list, its paid tier needs an international bank card that most
Cameroonian students and small firms do not have, and it has no idea what an
ENSP engineering degree or a BTS in civil engineering actually is.

Afiri was our answer: a platform for the people both sides of that gap.

## My part in it

This was a **team of twenty-one**, organised into eight poles, and it is worth
being precise about which parts were mine.

**I led the business, finance, market and HR work.** Market and competitor
analysis, the two client personas, the business model canvas, the revenue model,
three-year financial projections and the break-even calculation, the choice of
legal structure under OHADA, and the HR and recruitment plan. When the first
pricing grid turned out not to cover our own cost base, I reworked it — that is
the reason the published tiers are 30k/65k/120k FCFA rather than the 10k/20k/30k
we started from. I was also one of the three people who wrote the final report.

**On the technical side I was a member of the back-end pole**, worked with the
security pole, reviewed the front end and made small corrections to it — and I
built the **admin analytics dashboard** on my own.

## The dashboard

The dashboard is a single self-contained page served by FastAPI at `/dashboard`.
It signs in through the API's own OAuth2 endpoint, holds the JWT in memory
rather than in storage, and then drives five KPI counters — total users, students,
companies, active listings, applications — from five analytics endpoints. Two
Chart.js visualisations sit underneath: a doughnut splitting students from
companies, and a horizontal bar chart of the five sectors posting the most roles.
Everything re-fetches on a visible 30-second countdown.

The part I care about most is not the charts, it's the access rule behind them.
**Every analytics endpoint checks the caller's role before it touches the
database** and returns 403 to anyone who is not an administrator. An earlier
version did not, and the security audit caught it — fixing it meant the numbers
on that page stopped being readable by any logged-in student.

## What the platform does

Students sign up, build a mini-CV — name, degree, university, skills, projects —
and get a **short share link** that a recruiter can open without an account.
They browse listings filtered by field, city and contract type, apply in one
click, and track each application's status. A four-question quiz maps their
answers onto one of ten fields and returns suggested roles, with the answers
persisted so the result is still there on the next visit.

Companies publish listings and get a dashboard counting applications per
listing. Ownership is enforced server-side throughout: a company can only ever
read or modify **its own** listings and the applications attached to them.

## Honest notes

It was built in **two weeks**, and it is an MVP in the strict sense — messaging,
email notifications, password recovery and a native mobile app were all
deliberately scoped out and documented as such rather than half-built.

The team also ran a **security audit on its own work** and published the result:
fifteen findings, each with a severity rating and a named remediation. Some are
fixed; the remaining ones sit at the top of the roadmap. Auditing your own
project and writing down what you found is uncomfortable, and I think it is the
single most professional thing we did.

## What I took from it

Coordinating twenty-one people across eight poles was harder than any individual
piece of the code. What made it work was daily check-ins and a shared repository
— and what nearly broke it was the gap between poles, where everyone assumes
someone else owns the join.

The other lesson was commercial, and it surprised me: **our first pricing model
was wrong, and only the financial projection revealed it.** Building the
three-year model was not an academic exercise to satisfy a brief — it was the
thing that showed the platform could not pay for itself at the prices we had
picked. I would not have found that in the code.

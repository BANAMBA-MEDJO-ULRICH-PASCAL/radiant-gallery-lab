## The problem

Most of CAMRAIL's network is **single track**. That one fact makes railway
scheduling genuinely hard: two trains travelling in opposite directions on the
same section don't just inconvenience each other, they cannot both exist there.
Every crossing has to be planned into a passing loop in advance.

Plan a dozen circulations across that corridor by hand and the combinations get
away from you quickly. And the moment one train is 45 minutes late, the plan you
spent hours building is wrong — not slightly wrong, but wrong in a cascade, as
each knocked-back train collides with the next one's reserved slot.

## The approach

**Model the network properly first.** Stations, sections, passing loops, platform
capacity and rolling-stock rotations are all represented explicitly, because a
conflict you haven't modelled is a conflict you cannot detect.

**Then classify the conflicts.** The simulator detects eight distinct types, each
with its own detection rule and its own explanatory page in the interface:
head-on crossing, overtaking, catching up, passing-loop avoidance,
platform-at-station contention, equipment-rotation conflicts, resource
unavailability, and cascade effects where one delay propagates through the day.

**Reduce the scheduling to graph colouring.** This is the heart of it. Build a
graph in which every node is a train movement and every edge joins two movements
that cannot happen at once. A valid conflict-free timetable is then exactly a
**proper colouring** of that graph — adjacent nodes must differ, and each colour
becomes a safe time slot.

I implemented two classic heuristics so their behaviour could be compared on the
same network: **Welsh–Powell**, which colours greedily in descending degree
order, and **DSATUR**, which picks the most saturated vertex next and generally
uses fewer colours — meaning a tighter timetable.

**Then break it on purpose.** The simulator injects a delay on any chosen train
from any chosen station and recalculates the entire plan, so you can watch the
knock-on effects propagate instead of theorising about them.

## The interface

The backend serves the site directly: a Leaflet map of the real corridor,
twelve circulations colour-coded by service type (Express, Semi-direct, Freight,
Night), playback from ×4 to ×60, and a panel to declare an incident and watch the
network respond.

There are also dedicated pages explaining the project's rationale and the
methodology behind it — because a simulator a dispatcher can't interpret isn't
much use, however correct the algorithm underneath.

## What I took from it

The real lesson was that the hard part isn't the algorithm. Graph colouring is
textbook. The hard part is **modelling the domain accurately enough that the
algorithm is solving the right problem** — getting passing loops, platform
occupancy and equipment rotations into the graph correctly. A beautifully
coloured graph of the wrong constraints is a very convincing wrong answer.
